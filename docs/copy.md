Quick answer: for a small local business, most SEO providers charge somewhere between $500 and $2,000 a month on a retainer, $75 to $150 an hour for hourly work, and $500 to $5,000 for a one-time project. Local SEO tends to cost less than national SEO. In Ahrefs’ survey of 439 SEO providers, those serving local markets averaged $1,557 a month. Cheaper plans exist and can work for a contractor in a few towns. Expensive plans exist too, and many small businesses do not need them.

So how much does SEO cost for you specifically? It depends on how many towns you want to show up in, how crowded your trade is locally, and whether your website needs to be rebuilt first. Below are the real survey numbers, what pushes the price up or down, and the red flags that should end a sales call early.

### What the surveys say SEO costs

There is no official price list for SEO, so the best data comes from surveys of the people selling it. Two of the more useful ones:

- Ahrefs polled 439 SEO service providers (article first published December 2023, updated since) and checked each one’s website by hand to confirm they really sold SEO.
- SE Ranking published a 2025 agency pricing survey (December 2024) built from 260 agencies. 94% of them serve small or local businesses.
Here is how the numbers line up.

| Pricing model | Ahrefs (439 providers) | SE Ranking (260 agencies) |
|---|---|---|
| Monthly retainer, most common | $501–$1,000/month (20.4% of respondents) | $500–$1,000/month |
| Monthly retainer, spread | 68.8% charge $2,000/month or less | 64% charge under $1,000; 30% under $500 |
| Local SEO, monthly average | $1,557/month (agencies ~$1,819, freelancers ~$1,150) | Not reported separately |
| Hourly, most common | $75–$100/hour (24% of respondents) | $50–$100/hour |
| One-time project, most common | $2,501–$5,000 (21.2% of respondents) | $500–$2,000 |

#### Where the sources disagree

The surveys roughly agree on the most common monthly band ($500 to $1,000). They split on almost everything else, and there are good reasons:

- Who they asked. SE Ranking’s sample skewed toward small agencies (78% had under 10 people) and included many European shops, which charge less. Ahrefs’ pool included consultants and big agencies, and 79.1% of its U.S. and Canada providers charge at least $1,001 a month.
- How averages were figured. Ahrefs calculated its averages by assuming every respondent charges the top of their price bracket, so the averages lean high.
- Sample size. SE Ranking says outright that its sample “may not be large enough to reach statistical significance.”
- Ahrefs disagrees with itself in spots. Its summary says 23% charge $500 to $1,000 a month, while the detailed section says 20.4% charge $501 to $1,000. Small difference, but it shows these numbers are estimates, not gospel.
The fair takeaway: a U.S. local business will most often be quoted somewhere between $500 and $2,000 a month, and anything outside that range deserves a clear explanation of why.

#### How SEO is billed

BrightLocal’s industry survey found that a monthly fee based on deliverables is the most popular way to bill for local SEO: 63% of agencies and 51% of freelancers use it. Per-project billing came next (36% of agencies, 44% of freelancers), then hourly (28% of agencies, 43% of freelancers). Plenty of shops mix models, like a setup project followed by a monthly plan.

### What drives the price of local SEO

Two HVAC companies can get quotes that are $1,500 a month apart, and both quotes can be fair. Here is what moves the number.

How crowded your market is. A pool service in one quiet town has a different fight than a roofer trying to rank in a big metro after a storm season. More competitors with strong profiles and hundreds of reviews means more work to catch up.

How many towns and services you want to rank for. Showing up for “plumber Wellington” is one job. Showing up for drain cleaning, water heaters, and repiping across eight towns is a much bigger one. Each town and service usually needs its own well-built page.

Content. Writing real service pages and city pages (not the same page with the town name swapped) takes time. Plans that include regular new content cost more.

Links and citations. Getting your business listed accurately across directories is fairly cheap. Earning real links from local organizations, suppliers, and news sites takes more effort and costs more.

Whether a website is included. If your current site is five thin pages on a builder you do not control, part of what you are paying for is a rebuild. Some agencies fold that into the monthly fee. Others charge it up front. Either way, ask.

Who does the work. In Ahrefs’ data, agencies charged 138% more than freelancers on average. You pay for more people and more process. Sometimes that is worth it and sometimes it is overhead.

### What a small contractor actually needs (and what gets upsold)

Most local service businesses win or lose on the Google Maps results and a handful of searches in their own area. Google says local results are “mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity”, and that there is “no way to request or pay for a better local ranking.” So the work that matters is the work that makes you more relevant and more trusted in your area.

What most contractors need:

- Someone checking in each month on what changed and what to do next
What often gets upsold:

- National keyword campaigns when you only work in one county
- Blog posts every week on topics nobody in your area searches
- Social media “SEO” packages that have little to do with Google rankings
- Big link-building bundles from sites that have nothing to do with your trade or town
- Reports stuffed with vanity numbers instead of calls and form fills

### Red flags before you sign an SEO contract

Google has a page on hiring an SEO that every owner should skim. Its bluntest line: “No one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google.” Watch for these:

- Guaranteed #1 rankings. Google says to be wary of anyone who claims to guarantee rankings or have a “special relationship” with Google.
- Long lock-in contracts. A 12-month contract with a big cancellation fee protects the agency, not you. Good work keeps clients without a trap.
- The agency owns your website or profile. If the domain is registered in their name, or they are the only owner on your Google Business Profile, you can lose both when you leave. On a Business Profile, only owners can add or remove users. Managers cannot. You should be an owner.
- Vague reports. If you cannot tell what was done last month and why, you are paying for a mystery. Google’s own guide says your SEO should share every change they make to your site and explain the reasoning.
- Secret methods. Google warns against SEOs who are secretive or will not clearly explain what they plan to do. If they create deceptive content on your behalf, you are the one who pays for it.
- Cold emails promising page one. Google says to treat unsolicited SEO emails with the same skepticism as “burn fat at night” diet pills.

Here is how to get more Google reviews as a home service business: ask every customer right after the job is done, text them your direct Google review link while you are still in the driveway, follow up once if they don’t leave one, and reply to every review you get. Then automate the ask so it happens after every job, not just when someone remembers.

That is the whole system. The rest of this guide is the details that make it work, plus the rules that keep your Google Business Profile out of trouble.

### Quick answer: the 8-step review checklist

- Ask when the job is finished and the customer is happy. The AC is blowing cold, the leak is fixed, the pavers are sealed.
- Get your direct review link from your Google Business Profile (steps below).
- Text the link, don’t just say “look us up on Google.”
- Make it one tap. The link should open the review form, not your website.
- Follow up once, a day or two later, then stop.
- Ask every customer. No filtering out the ones you think are unhappy.
- Train your techs to say the ask out loud, the same way every time.
- Reply to every review, good or bad, within a few days.

### Why Google reviews matter for Google Maps

Google says local results are based on three things: relevance, distance, and prominence. On its local ranking help page, Google describes prominence as how well known a business is, based partly on “how many reviews you have,” and says “more reviews and positive ratings can help your business’s local ranking.”

Notice the word “help.” Reviews are one input, not a switch. You still need the right categories, a complete profile, and a real service area. We saw this firsthand with Next Level Air Conditioning: it had 31 five-star reviews and still did not show up in the top 20 Google Maps results in 4 live searches in its own area in August 2026. Its profile had 1 photo, no description, no services, and 1 category. Reviews could not carry that on their own.

So think of reviews as two jobs at once. They feed prominence for ranking, and they are what a homeowner reads before they decide to call you or the next company on the list. If you want the ranking side in full, read our guide on how to rank higher on Google Maps.

### Step 1: Ask at the right moment

The best time to ask for a review is the moment the customer feels relief. For an HVAC tech, that is when the house starts cooling down. For a plumber, it is when the water heater is back on. For a paver crew, it is the final walkthrough.

Wait until the next week and that feeling is gone. The customer is back to their busy life and your text sits unread.

A simple script works:

“Glad we got that fixed for you. We’re a local company and Google reviews really help people find us. I’m going to text you a link right now. If you have 30 seconds, an honest review would mean a lot.”

Keep it honest. You are asking for a review, not a five-star review. That wording matters for Google’s rules, which we cover below.

### Step 2: Get your direct Google review link

Stop telling people to “search for us on Google and leave a review.” That is four or five steps too many, and half of them will find a competitor on the way.

Google gives every Business Profile a direct link that opens the review form. According to Google’s help page on review links, here is how to get it on a computer:

- Go to your Business Profile (sign in at business.google.com, or search your business name on Google while signed in).
- Select Read reviews, then Get more reviews.
- Select Copy to copy the review link.
The same screen has a QR code. Google notes the QR code can only be generated from a computer browser, not a phone. Save it and put it on invoices, leave-behind cards, and truck magnets.

Save the link in your phone as a text shortcut so every tech can paste it in two seconds.

### Step 3: Make it one tap

Text beats email for most home service customers. They already have your number from scheduling, and a text gets read. Keep the message short and put the link on its own line:

“Hi Maria, it’s Luis from Coastal Air. Thanks for having us out today. If you have a minute, would you leave us an honest Google review? It helps a small local company a lot. [your review link]”

One link. No “also follow us on Facebook.” No survey first. Every extra step loses people.

### Step 4: Follow up once, then stop

Plenty of people mean to leave a review and forget. One friendly reminder a day or two later is fair:

“Hi Maria, just a quick reminder in case the link got buried. No worries if you’re busy. [your review link]”

Then stop. A second or third reminder turns a happy customer into an annoyed one.

### Step 5: Ask every customer (no review gating)

This is where a lot of contractors get into trouble without realizing it.

“Review gating” means sending a quick survey first, then only sending the happy customers to Google and routing unhappy ones to a private feedback form. Some review tools used to sell this as a feature. Google does not allow it.

Google’s Prohibited and restricted content policy says businesses must not “discourage or prohibit negative reviews, or selectively solicit positive reviews from customers.” The same policy bans offering incentives “such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services” in exchange for posting any review. Google’s tips for getting more reviews adds that incentivized reviews count as fake content and can lead to restrictions on your profile.

In plain terms:

| Allowed | Not allowed |
|---|---|
| Asking every customer for an honest review | Only asking customers you think are happy |
| Asking right after the job is done | Surveys that send only 4 and 5 star answers to Google |
| Putting your QR code on an invoice | “$20 off your next service for a review” |
| One polite follow-up | Gift card drawings for reviewers |
| Replying to a bad review | Pressuring someone to delete a bad review |

Asking everyone also works in your favor. Google’s own tips point out that a mix of positive and negative reviews feels more trustworthy. A profile of nothing but perfect five-star reviews can look staged to a homeowner.

### Step 6: Train your techs to ask

Your techs are the ones standing in the customer’s kitchen when the job wraps up. If the ask depends on the office remembering to send something three days later, it mostly won’t happen.

Make it part of the job close-out, like collecting payment:

- Give every tech the same short script (the one in Step 1 works).
- Have them send the text before they pull out of the driveway.
- Ask customers to mention what was done if they’re willing (“the tech,” “the water heater,” “the drain line”). Specific reviews read as real, and they tell future customers what you actually do.
- Track reviews by tech name when customers mention them, and call out the wins at your team meeting.
No bonuses tied to customers leaving five stars, though. That pushes techs toward the pressure tactics Google bans.

### How to respond to Google reviews

Replying is the half of the review system most contractors skip. Google’s review management page confirms that your reply is posted publicly under the review and that the reviewer is notified when you reply. On its local ranking page, Google also says “positive reviews and helpful replies can help your business stand out.”

To reply: open your Business Profile, select Read reviews, select Reply next to the review, write your response, and select Reply again. Google checks replies against its content policies before they appear, which is usually quick but can take longer.

Google’s tips for better replies boil down to a few rules that fit home service work well:

- Keep it short and simple. Two to four sentences.
- Use their name and mention the specific job.
- Skip the sales pitch. A reply is not an ad.
- Never share private details about the customer or the job.
- Apologize when it’s warranted, and explain honestly when something was out of your control.
- Reply in a timely way. Aim for within a few days.
Here are three examples for a home service business.

#### Replying to a 5-star review

“Thanks, Maria. Glad Luis got your AC cooling again before the weekend heat. Those capacitor failures always seem to happen on the hottest day. We appreciate you trusting a local company.”

Specific, warm, done. Mentioning the job naturally tells the next reader what kind of work you do.

#### Replying to a 3-star review

“Thanks for the honest feedback, Tom. You’re right that we arrived later than the window we gave you, and we should have called ahead. We’re glad the water heater install went smoothly once we got there. We’ve changed how our dispatcher sends arrival updates so that doesn’t happen again.”

A 3-star review usually means “the work was fine, something else bugged me.” Name that thing, own it, and say what changed.

#### Replying to a 1-star review

“Hi Denise, we’re sorry about your experience. We take this seriously and want to make it right. Please call our office at [phone] and ask for [owner name] so we can go over what happened with your drain line and find a fix.”

Stay calm, don’t argue in public, and take it offline. Never post invoice details, the address, or anything private, even if the review gets facts wrong. Future customers judge you more on how you reply than on the complaint itself.

If a review breaks Google’s policies (fake, spam, a competitor, someone who was never a customer), you can report it through your profile. Don’t report a review just because it’s negative.

### Step 7: Automate the ask after every job

Everything above works. The problem is doing it every single time when you have three trucks out, a missed call from a new lead, and a supplier on hold.

That is where a CRM helps. When a job is marked complete, the system texts the customer your review link automatically, sends one reminder if no review comes in, and then stops. You can also get a notification when a new review posts so replies don’t slip through.

Automation doesn’t change the rules. It still asks every customer and still asks for an honest review. It just removes “we forgot” from the equation.

- Asking by email only. Many homeowners never open it.
- Sending people to your website first. Send the direct review link.
- Asking once a quarter in a big blast. A steady pace after each job looks natural and keeps your profile fresh.
- Ignoring reviews for months. Unanswered negative reviews do the most damage.
- Buying reviews or trading reviews with other businesses. This is fake engagement under Google’s policy and can get reviews removed or your profile restricted.

To rank higher on Google Maps, you need Google to be sure of three things: that your business matches the search (relevance), that you’re close enough to the person searching (distance), and that you’re a well-known, trusted business (prominence). Google says so plainly in its own help doc on how local results are determined. You can’t control distance. You can control almost everything about relevance and prominence, and that’s where the work is.

### Quick answer: how to rank higher on Google Maps

- Pick the right primary category, plus a few accurate secondary categories.
- Fill in every service you offer, with plain descriptions.
- Set your service areas to the towns you actually work in.
- Keep hours, phone, and website link correct.

- Ask every happy customer for a review, and reply to all of them.

### What Google actually ranks on

Google’s help page lists three factors. Here is what each one means for a contractor.

Relevance is “how well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for.” If someone searches “water heater repair” and your profile says “Plumber” with no services listed, Google has to guess. Categories, services, your description, and your website all feed relevance.

Distance is “how far each business is from the customer who’s searching.” If the searcher doesn’t share their location, Google uses what it knows about where they are. This one is out of your hands. Your address is your address.

Prominence is “how well-known a business is.” Google says this is based partly on “how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have,” and that “more reviews and positive ratings can help your business’s local ranking.” Reviews, links, mentions around the web, and how well your website ranks in regular search all build prominence.

Google also says there’s no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. Ads are separate.

#### The map pack changes block by block

This is the part most owners don’t hear. Because distance is baked in, there isn’t one “Google Maps ranking” for your business. There’s a different one from every street in town.

An HVAC company in Royal Palm Beach might sit at #2 for “AC repair” when someone searches from the shopping plaza down the road, then drop to #9 for a homeowner in Wellington a few miles away, and not show at all from Jupiter. Same business, same day, same search.

So when you check your ranking from your own phone at the shop, you’re seeing your best-case result. A fair picture takes searches from many points across your service area. That’s how we measure it, and it’s why we never promise a single rank number.

The honest goal is not “be #1.” It’s “show up in the top 3 across more of the map, for the services that make you money.”

### The action plan for home service businesses

#### 1. Get your categories right

Your primary category carries the most weight. Choose the one that describes what your business is: “Air conditioning contractor,” “Plumber,” “Roofing contractor,” “Pest control service.” Google’s profile guidelines say to use as few categories as possible to describe your core business, and to pick specific ones over broad ones.

Then add secondary categories only for real parts of your business. An HVAC shop that also does ductwork and heating might add “HVAC contractor” and “Heating contractor.” Don’t add “Electrician” because you once swapped a breaker.

Check what the top 3 in your town use as their primary category. If all three say “Air conditioning repair service” and you say “Contractor,” you’re fighting uphill.

#### 2. List every service

In the Services section, add each thing you do as its own line: AC repair, AC installation, duct cleaning, maintenance plans, mini split installation. Write a sentence or two for each in plain words a homeowner would use. This feeds relevance for searches your category alone doesn’t cover.

#### 3. Set service areas honestly

If you go to customers and don’t take walk-ins, you’re a service-area business. Google’s guidelines say to hide your address in that case and list the areas you serve instead. Google’s service area help page allows up to 20 service areas and says your overall area shouldn’t be more than about 2 hours of driving time from where the business is based.

Setting service areas doesn’t make you rank 40 miles away. Distance still applies. It tells customers where you work.

#### 4. Keep the basics accurate

Hours, phone number, website link, and business name. Your name must be your real business name, with no added keywords or city names. Google’s guidelines say not to include keywords or taglines in the name. If you offer 24/7 emergency service, set your hours that way only if someone really answers.

#### 5. Add real photos

Upload photos of actual jobs, your trucks, your crew, and before-and-after work. Google’s own ranking tips include adding photos and videos. A profile with one blurry logo tells a homeowner nothing. Aim to add a few new ones every month.

#### 6. Post updates

Profile posts let you share a seasonal offer, a recent job, or a service reminder (“Hurricane season: get your roof checked before June”). They keep the profile active and give customers another reason to call you.

#### 7. Get reviews and reply to every one

Ask every satisfied customer, every time, with a direct link. Reply to every review, good or bad. Google lists responding to reviews as one of its tips for local ranking.

Stay inside the rules. Google’s review policy bans offering incentives such as discounts or free service in exchange for reviews, and bans selectively asking only happy customers (“review gating”). Our guide on how to get more Google reviews covers systems that stay inside those lines.

#### 8. Build a page for each service and each town

Your website is part of your Google Maps ranking. Google’s prominence factor includes links to your business, and in our experience the site your profile links to matters a lot for relevance too. A 5-page site with one “Services” page can’t compete with a competitor who has a real page for “AC repair in Wellington” and “AC installation in Lake Worth Beach.”

Each page should be written for that service or town: the problems you fix there, neighborhoods you work in, real job photos, and a clear phone number. Not the same page with the city name swapped. For more on this, see our guide to local SEO for contractors.

#### 9. Clean up citations

Citations are listings of your name, address, and phone on other sites: Yelp, Angi, the BBB, Apple Maps, Bing Places, Nextdoor, your trade association. Get them consistent with your Google profile. Old phone numbers and old addresses floating around the web confuse customers and don’t help Google trust your details.

#### 10. Earn local links

A link from your local chamber of commerce, a supplier’s dealer locator, a youth sports team you sponsor, or a local news story is worth more than a pile of junk directory links. Google names links to your business as part of prominence. These take time. They’re also hard for competitors to copy.

### Reviews alone are not enough

Plenty of owners assume reviews are the whole game. They’re a big part of prominence, but they can’t make up for weak relevance.

Here’s a real example. In August 2026, Next Level Air Conditioning, a Lake Worth HVAC company, had 31 five-star reviews. In 4 live searches in its own area, it did not appear in the top 20 Google Maps results. Its profile had 1 photo, no description, no services, and 1 category. Its website had 5 pages.

Thirty-one perfect reviews and still invisible. Google simply didn’t have enough to connect that business to the searches homeowners were making. In September 2026 we rebuilt the site to 30+ pages (including 15 city pages) and rebuilt the profile (categories, description, service areas). Results are still in progress, so we’re not claiming any ranking gains yet. The point stands: reviews without relevance don’t get you on the map.

### Why is my business not showing up on Google Maps?

If you can’t find your business at all, ranking isn’t the problem yet. Something is blocking the listing. The common causes:

- The profile isn’t verified. Unverified profiles often don’t show, or show with limited info. Finish verification in your Business Profile dashboard.
- The profile is suspended. Google can suspend profiles that break its guidelines. Keyword-stuffed names, virtual office addresses, and multiple profiles for one business are frequent triggers. You’ll need to fix the problem and appeal.
- Service-area settings are off. If you hid your address but set no service areas, or set areas far from where you’re based, you may show up in fewer places than you expect.
- Category mismatch. If your primary category is “General contractor” and you’re searching “roof repair,” Google may not see you as relevant to that search.
- You’re searching from too far away. You search “plumber” from Boca and wonder why your Jupiter shop isn’t there. That’s distance at work, not a broken listing. Search your exact business name first. If it shows, the listing is live.
- Your listing is being filtered as a duplicate. Google’s guidelines say there should be only one profile per business. If there’s an old duplicate (from a previous address, a former partner, or a listing someone else created), Google may show one and hide the other. Find the duplicates and have them merged or removed.

Distance is how far you are from the searcher. Prominence is how well known you are, which Google says includes links to your site and your reviews. Everything below feeds one of those three.

### Quick answer: the local SEO checklist for contractors

- Pick the right primary category on your Google Business Profile and add only the secondary categories that truly fit.
- Fill in every service you offer, a real description, hours, and service areas.
- Upload real job photos from your own trucks, crews, and projects, and keep adding them.

### Step 1: Build out your Google Business Profile

For most contractors this is the biggest lever, and it is free. It is also where most profiles are weakest.

#### Categories

Your primary category tells Google what you are. It matters a lot. In Whitespark’s 2026 Local Search Ranking Factors survey, local SEO practitioners ranked the primary GBP category as the number one factor for map pack rankings.

Pick the category that finishes the sentence “This business is a…” A paver company is likely “Paving contractor” or “Landscape designer,” not “Contractor.” An AC company should look at “Air conditioning contractor” or “HVAC contractor.” Google’s profile guidelines say to use as few categories as possible to describe your core business, so add secondary categories only for real lines of work (for example, “Air duct cleaning service” if you actually do it).

A good way to choose: search your main service in your town, open the top three profiles, and see which primary category they use.

#### Services

The services section is where you list every job you want calls for. A plumber should not stop at “Plumbing.” List drain cleaning, water heater installation, tankless water heaters, leak detection, repiping, sewer line repair, and so on. Add a short, plain description to each one. This is free relevance, and most of your competitors skip it.

#### Service areas

If you go to the customer’s home and don’t take walk-ins, you are a service-area business. Google says service-area businesses should hide their address from customers. Then add the towns you actually serve. Google’s guidelines also say your service area generally shouldn’t extend farther than about 2 hours of driving from where you are based.

One honest note: service areas tell Google where you work, but they do not cancel out distance. A company based in Royal Palm Beach will usually have an easier time in the Wellington map pack than in Boca Raton. That is why town pages on your website (Step 2) matter so much.

#### Photos

Upload real photos: finished jobs, before and afters, your trucks with the logo, your crew in uniform, your shop. Skip stock photos. Homeowners can tell, and real photos show you do this work in their area. Add a few every month, not 50 once and never again.

#### Posts

Google posts are short updates on your profile. Use them for a finished job (“New paver driveway in Lake Worth Beach”), a seasonal reminder (“AC tune-up before summer”), or an offer. They are not a big ranking lever, but they show the profile is active and give people a reason to call.

#### Reviews

Reviews help with both ranking and getting picked. Google’s own page says more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking.

The trick is making it routine. Ask at the end of every job, while the customer is happy and you are still in the driveway. Follow up the same day with a text that has your direct review link. Reply to every review, good and bad, with a real sentence, not a copy-paste.

Reviews alone are not enough, though. Next Level Air Conditioning, a Lake Worth HVAC company, had 31 five-star reviews and still did not appear in the top 20 Google Maps results in 4 live searches in its own area in August 2026. The profile had 1 photo, no description, no services, and 1 category, and the website had 5 pages. In September 2026 we rebuilt the site to 30+ pages, including 15 city pages, and rebuilt the profile’s categories, description, and service areas. Results are still in progress, but the lesson is already clear: good reviews can’t carry an empty profile.

### Step 2: Build a website with a page per service and main town

Your profile links to your website, and Google reads that site to figure out what you do and where. A five-page site with “Services” as one long list gives Google very little to work with.

#### One page per service

Each main service gets its own page. A roofer might have separate pages for roof replacement, roof repair, tile roofing, metal roofing, flat roofs, and storm damage. Each page should cover:

- What the job involves and when a homeowner needs it
- What affects the price (without making up numbers you can’t back up)
- Photos of that type of job you actually did
- A few FAQs you hear on the phone
- A clear phone number and a short form

#### One page per main town

Then build a page for each town that brings in real work. Not 200 thin pages with the city name swapped. A good town page mentions the neighborhoods you work in, the kinds of homes there (older homes with cast iron drains, newer builds with HOA rules on pavers), jobs you have done nearby, and reviews from customers in that town.

This is what worked for Hero’s Pavers, a Lake Worth Beach paver and hardscape company. After an SEO website rebuild and Google Business Profile optimization, it now ranks #2 to #3 on Google for multiple Lake Worth keywords.

#### The basics that still matter

- Title tags that say the service and town (“Paver Driveways in Wellington, FL | Company Name”)
- Your phone number as a tap-to-call link on every page
- A fast site on phones, since that is where most homeowners search
- Your business name, address, and phone in the footer, matching your profile exactly

### Step 3: Keep your name, address, and phone consistent (NAP and citations)

NAP means name, address, phone. Citations are listings of your business on other sites: Yelp, BBB, Angi, Nextdoor, Facebook, Apple Maps, Bing Places, your chamber directory, and trade directories.

The goal is simple: the same name, address, and phone everywhere. Problems show up when you moved shops, changed phone numbers, or rebranded, and old listings still show the old info. That sends mixed signals and sends some customers to a dead number.

Do this once:

- Write down your exact business name, address, and main phone as they appear on your profile.
- Search your business name and old phone numbers on Google.
- Fix or claim every listing that is wrong.
- Get listed on the big ones you are missing, plus any trade-specific directories (manufacturer dealer locators are a good one).

### Step 4: Earn local links

Links from other websites are part of prominence, and Google names them directly. For a contractor, the best links are local and real, not bought from a link seller overseas.

Places to start:

- Chamber of commerce. Join and make sure your member listing links to your site.
- Suppliers and manufacturers. Many run “find a dealer” or “certified installer” pages. If you are a certified installer for a brand, ask to be listed.
- Sponsorships. Youth sports teams, school events, charity 5Ks. Sponsors usually get a logo and a link.
- Local groups. BNI chapters, trade associations, HOA vendor lists.
- Partners. A pool company and a paver company refer each other all the time. A “trusted partners” page on each site makes sense.
A handful of links from real businesses in your county is worth more than a pile of junk links from sites no one visits.

### Step 5: Track your calls

If you can’t tell which calls came from Google, you can’t tell if any of this is working.

- In your Google Business Profile, check the performance section for calls, direction requests, and website clicks.
- Use a call tracking number on your website so calls from organic search are counted. Keep your main number as the primary number on your profile so your NAP stays consistent.
- Make sure form submissions land somewhere you will see them fast and that each one gets answered.
- Ask every new customer “How did you find us?” and write it down.
Tracking also shows you where leads slip away. A missed call on a Saturday is often a customer calling the next contractor on the list. A missed-call text-back closes most of that gap.

### What NOT to do

Some tricks work for a while, then get your profile suspended. When a contractor’s profile disappears, it is often one of these:

- Stuffing keywords into your business name. “ABC Plumbing: Best Emergency Plumber Palm Beach” is against Google’s rules. Google says adding unnecessary info to your name can get your profile suspended. Keywords in the name do seem to help rankings, which is why people do it, and it is also why competitors report it. Use your real name.
- Fake addresses. A UPS Store box, a virtual office, or a buddy’s address in a town you want to rank in does not qualify. Google’s guidelines say P.O. boxes, remote mailboxes, and virtual offices aren’t acceptable.
- Buying reviews or trading discounts for them. Google’s policy bans reviews that are paid for, directly or in kind, including discounts or free service for a review.
- Review gating. Only asking happy customers, or sending unhappy ones to a private form first, is also against the same policy. Ask everyone.
- Hundreds of copy-paste city pages. Pages with only the town name swapped in rarely help and can drag the rest of the site down. Write fewer, better town pages.
- Paying anyone who guarantees rankings. Google itself says there is no way to pay for a better local ranking. We don’t guarantee rankings either. Nobody honest can.

## Speed to Lead: How Contractors Can Answer Every New Lead in Minutes

Speed to lead for contractors comes down to one habit: the homeowner hears back from you before they hear back from anyone else. Here is how to make that happen when you are on a roof, under a house, or driving between jobs.

Speed to lead is the time between a homeowner reaching out (a call, a web form, a text, a message on your Google profile) and your business giving them a real response. For contractors, the shorter that gap, the better your odds of getting the job. The homeowner with a dead AC or a leaking water heater is usually calling or messaging several companies at once, and the first one that answers tends to get the appointment.

### Quick answer: how to improve speed to lead

- Text back every missed call automatically, within seconds, so no caller hits a dead end.
- Auto-reply to every web form the moment it is submitted, by text and email.
- Put every lead in one shared inbox or CRM so nothing lives only in one person’s phone.
- Use an AI answering service for after-hours calls and for overflow when you are busy.
- Run a short follow-up sequence for leads who do not answer the first time.
- Measure your response time every week and fix the gaps you find.
The rest of this guide explains why each step matters and how to set it up without hiring a full-time office person.

### Why speed to lead matters: what the research says

Two studies get quoted more than any others on this topic. Both are worth knowing, and both come with a caveat: they studied sales leads across industries like insurance, software, and financial services, not home service trades. Nobody has run the same study on plumbers or roofers. Still, the pattern is hard to ignore, and anyone who has lost a job to “we already hired someone” knows it holds up in the field.

#### The Harvard Business Review study (2011)

In “The Short Life of Online Sales Leads”, published in Harvard Business Review in March 2011, James B. Oldroyd, Kristina McElheran, and David Elkington audited 2,241 U.S. companies by sending each one a web-generated test lead. Here is what they found:

- 37% responded within an hour.
- 24% took more than 24 hours.
- 23% never responded at all.
- The average response time, among companies that responded within 30 days, was 42 hours.
In a separate study of 1.25 million sales leads, the same authors found that firms that tried to contact a lead within an hour were “nearly seven times as likely to qualify the lead” as those that waited even an hour longer, and “more than 60 times as likely” as companies that waited 24 hours or longer.

#### The Lead Response Management study (InsideSales.com and MIT, 2007)

The Lead Response Management study was done by InsideSales.com with Dr. James Oldroyd, then at MIT, and presented in October 2007. It looked at 3 years of data from six companies, covering more than 15,000 leads and over 100,000 call attempts. Its best-known finding: the odds of qualifying a lead dropped 21 times when the first call came at 30 minutes instead of 5 minutes. The study summary also reports that “the odds of contacting a lead if called in 5 minutes versus 30 minutes drop 100 times.”

These numbers are old and they are not about trades. What they show is direction, not a promise: the longer a lead waits, the colder it gets, and the drop starts within minutes, not days.

### Why contractors are slow to respond (and it is not laziness)

Most owners we talk to care a lot about calling people back. The problem is the job itself.

- You are on a roof. Your phone is in your truck or your pocket, and you are not stopping mid-tear-off to answer an unknown number.
- You are under a house or in an attic. No signal, or no free hand.
- You are driving. You see the call, you cannot safely take it, and by the time you park you have forgotten it.
- You are with a customer. Answering another call in the middle of a sales visit costs you that sale.
- It is after hours. Plenty of homeowners fill out forms at 9 p.m. after the kids are asleep. That lead sits until morning.
- The lead went to the wrong place. The form emails an old address, the Google message goes to someone’s personal phone, or the voicemail box is full.
None of these get fixed by trying harder. They get fixed with systems that answer for you in the first minute and put the lead in front of you when you are free.

### How to fix your speed to lead: 6 steps

#### 1. Set up missed-call text-back

This is the single biggest win for most contractors. When a call goes unanswered, the system sends a text to that caller within seconds. The caller knows a real business got their call, and most will reply by text with what they need. You can answer that text from the driveway between jobs.

A simple example:

Hi, this is Mike with [Your Company]. Sorry we missed your call, we are on a job right now. What can we help you with? If it is an emergency, reply URGENT and we will call you back right away.

Keep it short. Use a real first name. Give them one clear thing to do. Skip the links and the sales pitch.

Missed-call text-back is included in our $297/month plan as part of lead follow-up, along with review automation and Google Business Profile management.

#### 2. Auto-reply to every web form instantly

A homeowner who fills out your form and hears nothing assumes nobody saw it, and moves on to the next company. Set every form on your site (and any lead source that sends you forms) to trigger an instant text and email that:

- Confirms you got it, by name.
- Says when a person will reach out (“within the hour during business hours” is a promise you can keep).
- Asks one useful question, like a photo of the problem or the best time to call.
That photo question is a quiet win. You get a better sense of the job before you call, and the homeowner is now in a conversation with you, not your competitor.

#### 3. Put every lead in one shared inbox or CRM

If leads come in through calls, texts, web forms, Google Business Profile messages, and Facebook, and each one lands somewhere different, some will get lost. A shared inbox or simple CRM pulls them into one list. Anyone on your team (your spouse who handles the books, your office manager, your lead tech) can see who is waiting and who already got a reply.

The goal is simple: at any moment you can answer the question “who has reached out that nobody has talked to yet?”

#### 4. Use an AI answering service for after-hours and overflow calls

Text-back covers the caller who is fine texting. Some callers want a voice, especially with an emergency. An AI answering service picks up when you cannot: after hours, on weekends, or when two calls come in while you are already on one. It can take the caller’s name, address, and problem, answer basic questions about your service area and hours, and send you a summary so you call back already knowing the job.

It is not a replacement for you. It is the thing that keeps the lead warm until you can get to it. We quote AI answering service setups per project, since every business has different hours, service areas, and rules for what counts as urgent.

#### 5. Run a short follow-up sequence

Not every lead answers the first text. A light follow-up sequence catches the ones who got busy. Something like:

- Minute 0: Instant text-back or form reply.
- Hour 1: A second text if they have not replied (“Still need help with the water heater? We have an opening tomorrow morning.”).
- Day 1: A call from you or your office.
- Day 3: One last friendly check-in, then stop.
Keep it human and keep it short. Four touches over a few days is plenty for most home service leads. Stop the moment they reply or ask you to. If you need help building sequences that fit your trade, that is the kind of thing we set up through AI automation.

#### 6. Measure your response time every week

What gets measured gets fixed. Once a week, look at:

- Time to first response (automatic replies count, but track them separately).
- Time to first real conversation with a person.
- Leads with no response at all. This number should be zero.
- Where leads came from, so you know which sources send the most jobs.
Most CRMs will show these numbers. If yours does not, a spreadsheet with lead time and first-reply time for each lead still tells you a lot after a month.
